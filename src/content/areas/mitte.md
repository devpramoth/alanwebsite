Mitte is the hardest district to generalise about. Three very different kinds of building sit within a few streets of each other, and each one wants a different approach.

## The building stock

There's restored Altbau, much of it done to a high standard after 1990 and now due a second round. There's GDR-era Plattenbau — prefabricated panel construction, most obviously around Fischerinsel and the streets off Karl-Marx-Allee. And there's a lot of 1990s and 2000s infill: new-build flats dropped into the gaps left by war damage and the death strip.

The differences are significant in practice. Altbau is irregular, generous and full of surprises. New build is square, level and predictable, which makes fitting out fast but leaves nowhere to hide a change of mind. Plattenbau sits between the two — consistent and easy to plan, but with real limits on what you can cut into a structural panel and where the services are allowed to go.

## What I get called in for

A big share of the residential work here is turnaround — flats between tenancies or sales that need bringing up to standard quickly and to a set budget. That suits a planned programme, and it pays to be decisive about the specification early.

Beyond that, Mitte throws up more commercial work than anywhere else I operate. Retail fit-out, gallery installs, office and studio work, all with fixed dates and fixed access windows. That's the world I came out of. Wall builds and hanging systems for galleries, counters and display for retail, partitions and power for offices.

In the Plattenbau stock it's kitchens and bathrooms inside a fixed footprint. The layout generally can't move, so the job is about doing excellent work in the space that's already there.

## Practical points

Access is the defining constraint. Deliveries, parking and where a skip can stand in central Mitte all need real planning, and some streets only allow loading at certain hours. In commercial units the access window is usually set by the landlord or the centre management, not by me, and it drives the entire programme.

Buildings here are more likely than elsewhere to be owner-occupied with an active WEG, so consent for anything structural or anything affecting the common parts goes through a more formal process. Build that time into the plan.

Plenty of listed buildings too, and there the windows, facades and sometimes the interior features come with rules attached. Find that out early — it can decide what's worth designing at all.
